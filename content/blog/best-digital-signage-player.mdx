**Short answer:** for most small businesses with one to a few screens, an **Android signage box or the Amazon Signage Stick** is the simplest reliable choice. A **Raspberry Pi** is the cheapest option if you are comfortable managing it. **BrightSign** suits large, long-term networks where downtime is expensive. A regular **Fire TV Stick** is no longer a good choice for unattended screens (see [why](/blog/can-you-use-a-fire-stick-for-digital-signage/)).

*Last updated: 2 November 2026. Hardware prices have moved a lot this year, so confirm current prices before buying.*

## Pick by situation

| Your situation | Good fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One cafe or restaurant menu board | Signage Stick or Android signage box | Plug in, auto-starts, little upkeep |
| Small shop, 1 to 3 screens | Android signage box | Reliable and low cost |
| Tight budget, comfortable with setup | Raspberry Pi | Lowest hardware cost, but you manage SD cards and power |
| Many screens across locations | Android signage boxes or BrightSign | Remote management and consistency matter |
| Video wall or heavy web content | Mini PC or Chromebox | More processing power |
| Mission-critical, long lifespan | BrightSign | Purpose-built, with a track record in large networks |
| Just testing a platform | Whatever you already have | Do not buy hardware before you know the software fits |

## What a digital signage player does

A digital signage player is a small computer that plugs into a screen's HDMI port and plays the content your signage software sends it. Some screens have a player built in. Most small setups use an external one.

### Amazon Signage Stick

Launched by Amazon in late 2024 as a signage version of the Fire TV Stick. It boots straight into your signage software, supports 4K, and can be managed remotely through a compatible platform. It is only the player, so you still pay for signage software. Its main selling point over a regular Fire Stick is that it solves the auto-start problem. Read our [Amazon Signage Stick review](/blog/amazon-signage-stick-review-2025/) for more.

### Raspberry Pi

The Pi is popular because it is cheap, small, and flexible. It runs a full browser, which makes it a good fit for web-based players. The trade-offs:

- **SD cards fail**, particularly when power is cut without a clean shutdown. Use a quality card, a stable power supply, and consider a read-only setup.
- **Prices rose several times in 2026** because of memory costs, so the "$35 computer" idea no longer holds. Add a power supply, case, and card.
- **Performance limits.** Stick to 1080p H.264 video for smooth playback on most Pi hardware.

See our guide to the [best digital signage CMS for Raspberry Pi](/blog/best-digital-signage-cms-raspberry-pi/) for software options.

### Android signage box

An Android box built for signage is a reasonable middle ground: low cost, auto-start support, and enough power for typical menu boards and promotions. Avoid general-purpose TV streaming boxes for 24/7 use, since they are tuned for home viewing, not constant operation.

### Mini PC or Chromebox

Choose this when you need more processing power: video walls, 4K with several zones, or heavy web content. It costs more and uses more power.

### BrightSign

BrightSign players are purpose-built and widely used in large networks. They cost more up front, and they tie you more closely to BrightSign's ecosystem, so confirm that your signage software supports the model you plan to buy before ordering.

### Regular Fire TV Stick

Fine for testing or staffed screens. On FireOS 8 and later (February 2024 onward), apps cannot start automatically after a restart, so someone must relaunch the app by hand after each power cut. For unattended screens, choose a Signage Stick or an Android signage box instead. [Read the full explanation](/blog/can-you-use-a-fire-stick-for-digital-signage/).

## How to choose: seven questions

1. **Which signage software will you use?** Confirm it officially supports the player.
2. **Does the player start your app automatically after a power cut?**
3. **Can you manage and restart it remotely?**
4. **Ethernet or Wi-Fi?** Wired connections are more reliable for screens that must not drop out.
5. **What content will you play?** Video, 4K, portrait mode, and web content need different power levels.
6. **How many screens now, and in a year?**
7. **What is the total cost?** Include the player, power supply, cables, and the software subscription.

If a screen drops offline after you set it up, see [7 fixes for an offline digital signage player](/blog/digital-signage-player-offline-troubleshooting/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a separate media player for digital signage?**
Not always. Some commercial displays have a player built in, and some smart TVs can run a signage app directly. An external player is more reliable and easier to replace.

**Is a Raspberry Pi good enough for digital signage?**
Yes for small, simple setups, if you plan for SD card wear and power cuts. It is less suited to heavy video or large networks you cannot visit.

**What is the cheapest reliable digital signage player?**
Usually an Android signage box or a carefully set up Raspberry Pi. A regular Fire Stick looks cheapest, but it is unreliable for unattended screens.

**Is BrightSign worth the price?**
For large, long-term networks where downtime is costly, many buyers think so. For one or two screens, a cheaper player is usually enough.

**Can I use a smart TV without any player?**
Often yes, if the TV can run a signage app or a web player. Test it first, since TV apps can be slower and harder to manage remotely.

**What video format works best?**
MP4 with H.264 video and AAC audio at up to 1080p and 30fps plays smoothly on most low-power players, including Raspberry Pi.
